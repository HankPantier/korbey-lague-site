Most construction company owners think cost segregation is a real estate investor's tool. It shows up in articles about apartment complexes and commercial office buildings, and somewhere along the way, contractors decided it wasn't for them. That's a costly assumption. Construction firms are, in almost every measurable way, better candidates for cost segregation than the average real estate investor, and the ones who act on that fact are pulling forward tens of thousands of dollars in deductions that their competitors are waiting years to claim.

This isn't a tax loophole. It's an IRS-sanctioned engineering study that reclassifies components of a building or improvement from a 39-year depreciation schedule to 5-, 7-, or 15-year schedules. When you combine that with bonus depreciation, even in its current phase-down state, the front-loaded tax savings can be significant enough to change cash flow in the year the study is completed.

## Why construction companies are the ideal candidate

The logic is straightforward. Real estate investors buy buildings. Construction companies build them, renovate them, and in many cases, self-perform the work on their own facilities. That distinction matters for two reasons.

First, self-performed work creates detailed cost records. A contractor who builds his own shop knows exactly what the electrical rough-in cost, what the concrete flatwork ran, and how the HVAC was separated from the structure. That documentation is the raw material of a cost segregation study. A passive investor buying an existing building has to estimate. A contractor who built or renovated the facility often has line-item records already sitting in a job cost system.

Second, the types of assets common in construction operations are exactly the kinds of assets that reclassify well. Land improvements, specialty electrical systems, process-related plumbing, storage systems built into a facility, concrete pads for equipment, these are categories that routinely qualify for shorter depreciation lives. A general contractor who poured a reinforced slab specifically to support heavy machinery isn't just depreciating "a floor." That component may qualify as a 7-year asset, not a 39-year one.

Contractors and trades businesses also tend to carry high effective tax rates relative to their gross revenue because labor-intensive operations don't always generate the kind of passive income offsets that work in their favor. A cost segregation study directly attacks taxable income in years when it's most needed.

## What gets reclassified and what doesn't

A cost segregation study is conducted by an engineer, sometimes working alongside a tax professional, who reviews construction drawings, invoices, and site conditions to identify every component that can be assigned a shorter depreciation life under IRS guidance. The study produces a detailed report that your CPA then uses to amend prior returns or apply prospectively.

Here's a simplified view of the categories that typically come out of a study for a construction company facility:

| Asset Component | Default Life | Reclassified Life |
|---|---|---|
| Specialty electrical systems (equipment-specific) | 39 years | 5 or 7 years |
| Land improvements (parking, fencing, paving) | 39 years | 15 years |
| Decorative or removable interior finishes | 39 years | 5 or 7 years |
| Process-specific plumbing | 39 years | 5 or 7 years |
| Storage and shelving systems | 39 years | 5 or 7 years |
| Standard structural components | 39 years | 39 years |

Not everything reclassifies. The structural shell of the building, the roof membrane, and standard HVAC in non-specialized spaces generally stay at 39 years. The goal isn't to reclassify everything, it's to correctly identify what genuinely qualifies, document it defensibly, and apply the resulting deductions correctly.

## The bonus depreciation timing question

For the past several years, bonus depreciation allowed businesses to deduct 100% of the cost of qualifying assets in the year placed in service. That phase-down is now underway: 80% applied in 2023, 60% in 2024, 40% in 2025, and 20% in 2026, before bonus depreciation is currently scheduled to expire entirely under existing law (absent Congressional action).

That trajectory changes the calculus on timing. A cost segregation study completed on a 2024 or 2025 acquisition is still powerful, 60% and 40% first-year deductions on reclassified components are materially better than the straight-line alternative spread over 39 years. But the window is narrowing. A contractor who owned a facility for several years and has never done a study can still use a "look-back" study to catch up on missed depreciation, often without filing amended returns, using a change in accounting method (Form 3115). That's a meaningful option, though the prior-year bonus depreciation rates available at the original placed-in-service date would apply, not the current rate.

The practical implication: if a cost segregation study is something your construction business should consider, the time to evaluate it is now, not after the bonus depreciation rate drops again.

## When the numbers actually justify the cost

A cost segregation study isn't free. Engineering-based studies typically range from $5,000 to $15,000 depending on the size and complexity of the property. That means there's a threshold below which the economics don't work.

As a general rule, cost segregation tends to produce a strong return on investment for:

- New construction or major renovation projects with a cost basis above $500,000
- Acquisitions of existing commercial buildings or industrial facilities
- Build-outs of company-owned facilities where the contractor self-performed significant portions of the work
- Properties with substantial personal property or land improvement components (which is nearly every working contractor yard or shop)

For a construction company owner who built or renovated a 10,000-square-foot facility with a $1.2 million cost basis, it's not unusual for a study to identify $250,000–$400,000 in components eligible for reclassification. At a 37% federal rate, that's $90,000–$150,000 in accelerated deductions that would otherwise have been spread over nearly four decades. The study pays for itself many times over.

Entity structure also affects how those deductions flow. If the business is currently operating as an LLC taxed as a partnership, or as an S-corp, the depreciation deductions pass through to owners' individual returns, which is where the real tax rate calculation happens. If you haven't recently revisited your entity structure, that analysis belongs in the same conversation as cost segregation. ## Common questions

**Does cost segregation trigger an IRS audit?**
Not when it's done correctly. The IRS has published extensive guidance on cost segregation methodology, and a properly prepared engineering study with supporting documentation is a defensible position. Studies that are prepared by credentialed engineers following IRS guidelines, attached to properly filed returns or method change filings, are a recognized and accepted tax strategy. The risk comes from studies that are prepared without adequate documentation or that reclassify components that don't qualify. Working with a CPA firm that reviews the study output before it's applied to your return is the appropriate check on that risk.

**Can cost segregation apply to a building we purchased, not one we built?**
Yes. Acquired properties are eligible, and a look-back study can capture depreciation you've been under-claiming since the acquisition date. The reclassification applies to components that existed at the time of purchase and assigns them the correct depreciation life going forward, with a catch-up adjustment for prior years handled through a Form 3115 accounting method change rather than amended returns in most cases.

**Does cost segregation work if the building is owned by a separate LLC?**
Yes, and this is actually a common structure for contractor-owners who separate real estate from operating company risk. The depreciation deductions flow to the members of the holding LLC. If the operating company pays rent to the holding LLC, the study affects the holding entity's taxable income. How that flows to individual owners depends on the holding entity's tax classification, another reason entity structure analysis and cost segregation planning belong in the same conversation.

**What if we're planning to sell the building in a few years?**
Accelerated depreciation creates a depreciation recapture liability when the property is sold, taxed at up to 25% on the recaptured amounts. That doesn't make cost segregation a bad strategy, it changes the timing and structure of the analysis. In most cases, the time value of deferring tax and keeping cash in the business during the ownership period still outweighs the recapture at sale, especially when sale proceeds are being reinvested through a 1031 exchange. This is exactly the kind of scenario where having a CPA involved year-round, rather than just at filing time, protects you from surprises.
